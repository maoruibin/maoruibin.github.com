## Why I built another read-it-later app

Over the past few weeks I have been building a small tool called **Good Content**. The idea is simple: when you come across a worthwhile article — on WeChat, on a blog, on social media — you save it, and it truly becomes yours.

Most read-it-later services keep your articles on their servers. That is convenient until the service shuts down, the original page disappears, or the images quietly rot away. I have lost articles I cared about this way more than once. Good Content takes the opposite approach: everything lands on your device. The text is converted to Markdown, every image is downloaded to local storage, and the cloud — if you choose to use it at all — is your own drive, not somebody else's database.

## Reading is not skimming

The second half of the name matters as much as the first. Saving is easy; reading carefully is the hard part. So Good Content is built around close reading: you can highlight sentences in three line styles and five colors, attach a thought to any highlight, and write longer reflections at the end of an article. When your eyes are tired, the app can read the article aloud to you, sentence by sentence, while you walk or wash the dishes.

There is also an AI companion, but it is deliberately modest. You bring your own API key, and the AI only does what you ask: explain a difficult passage, outline the argument of a long essay. It never rewrites what the author wrote, and it never talks over the text.

## Your notes belong to you

Highlights and notes can be exported as clean Markdown, ready for Obsidian or Logseq. The whole library can be packed into a single archive and restored on a new phone in one step. "Data sovereignty" sounds like a grand phrase for a small app, but it is really just a promise: what you save stays yours.
